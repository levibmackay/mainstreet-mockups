# Making a photo-free page feel like *them*

Most of these businesses have no usable photography. Their site is a Hibu or
Wix template serving licensed stock, their Google listing says "Add a photo,"
and their Facebook is mostly faces we cannot use. After a full sourcing pass,
the majority of pages have zero or one image, and that is the honest ceiling.

So a page cannot rely on photography to feel personal. It has to earn that
some other way. This file is the how.

The failure this prevents: a beautifully typeset page that could be any
plumber in any town. The owner opens it, feels nothing, and does not reply.

## 1. Their logo (the biggest single win)

A logo is not a photograph. It has no faces, it is not licensed stock, it
comes from their own site, and it is the most identity-carrying asset a
business owns. Rule 4 permitted this from the start and it was simply missed
across all 23 sites.

Pull from their site favicon, header/footer lockup, `og:image`,
`apple-touch-icon`, or their own social profile image. Prefer SVG, then a
large transparent PNG.

Do not recolour, stretch, or crop the wordmark. If it was drawn for a white
background and your header is dark, build it a deliberate light plate rather
than letting it disappear.

Where a real logo exists, it replaces the drawn placeholder mark. Keep the
drawn mark only where nothing real could be found.

## 2. Their real brand colours

Sample the hex values straight off the logo. A page in a business's own
colours reads as theirs before a single word is read.

Do this deliberately, not mechanically. Each site in this repo was given a
divergent design direction on purpose, so the portfolio does not read as one
template reused. If their real colours fight the site's system, say so and
decide, rather than silently recolouring and flattening the whole set.

## 3. Radical specificity in the copy

This is the cheapest and most under-used lever, and it beats photography.

Generic: "Serving the local area for years."
Specific: "Sprinklers, dirt work and retaining walls in Rexburg, Sugar City
and St. Anthony since 2009."

Name the real towns. Name the real streets and landmarks where they are
verified. Use their real founding year, their real hours, their real service
radius, the real names of the services they actually sell in the words they
actually use. Every one of these must come off the verified fact sheet, and
rule 1 still outranks everything here: a specific invented detail is far
worse than a generic true one.

A page that names their town four times and lists the services they really
sell feels more like theirs than a stock photo of somebody else's van ever
will.

## 4. Draw what you cannot photograph

Custom SVG drawn *from* their reality is not a stock image and not a
fabrication:

- A real service-area map of the Upper Valley with their towns marked.
- A line illustration of their actual storefront or shop, drawn rather than
  photographed. This identifies them without republishing anyone's photo.
- Their actual equipment, product, or process, drawn as a diagram.
- Iconography built around what they specifically do, not generic trade
  clip-art. A garage-door company gets doors, not a wrench.

Drawn work is allowed to be ambitious. It is the main lever left once
photography is gone, so give it real effort rather than treating it as
filler.

## 5. Their real words

Where verified reviews or real copy from their own site exist, their own
phrasing carries more personality than anything written for them.

Do NOT add a rating, review count, or testimonial that is not already
verified, and do NOT remove one that is. See POLISH-BRIEF.md: a fact-check
pass on 2026-08-03 removed the unsupported claims and corrected the rest, so
anything still present survived that audit.

## 6. Turn the photo gap into the pitch

The honest, high-converting move: the page is missing their photos because we
would not use photos that are not theirs. Say that out loud in the outreach.

"I built this with what I could find publicly. It is missing your own photos,
which is the one thing I cannot get without you. Send me a handful of your
work and I will put them in."

That reframes the gap as respect for their business rather than an
incomplete job, and it asks for a small, easy yes that starts a conversation.
A reply with five photos attached is a warmer lead than a reply that says
"looks nice."

Design the page so those photos have somewhere to go: real slots in the
layout that improve when filled and do not look broken while empty. Never
ship an empty frame, a grey placeholder box, or an "image coming soon" state
on a page a prospect will see.

## What is still banned

Nothing here loosens rule 4. No stock, no AI-generated images, no photos of
another business, no licensed stock pulled off their own site, no
identifiable faces. If a technique above would require any of those, it is
not one of the techniques above.
